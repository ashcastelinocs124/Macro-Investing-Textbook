## Why this matters

In March 2020 the pandemic shut down the US economy almost overnight. Within weeks Congress passed the CARES Act, about $2.2 trillion of spending, tax breaks and loans, which included direct payments of $1,200 to households, extra unemployment benefits and loans to small businesses ([CRFB](https://www.crfb.org/blogs/whats-2-trillion-coronavirus-relief-package); [CRS](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R46729)). The federal deficit for that fiscal year came to $3.1 trillion, more than triple the year before ([CRS](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R46729)).

That is **fiscal policy**: the government using its budget to steer the economy. Chapter 4 covered the central bank's half of policy. This chapter covers the other half, and the question it raises: who pays for all of this, and when does debt become a problem?

## Taxes, spending, deficits and debt

Two definitions anchor this chapter:

- **[Fiscal policy](/Macro-Investing-Textbook/glossary/#fiscal-policy)** is "the use of government spending and taxation to influence the economy" ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/fandd/issues/Series/Back-to-Basics/Fiscal-Policy)). The government and the legislature set it through the budget. Monetary policy, set by the central bank, is the other half (see [Chapter 4](/Macro-Investing-Textbook/part-2-policy/04-central-banks/)).
- **[Government debt](/Macro-Investing-Textbook/glossary/#national-debt)**, also called public or national debt, is everything a government owes from borrowing over the years. The US Treasury defines its national debt as "the total amount of outstanding borrowing by the U.S. Federal Government accumulated over the nation's history" ([U.S. Treasury](https://fiscaldata.treasury.gov/americas-finance-guide/national-debt/)).

Governments take in money through taxes and spend it on things like pensions, health care, defence and interest on old debt.

- A **deficit** is what the government borrows in one year, when it spends more than it collects. A **surplus** is the opposite.
- **Debt** is the total pile built up by all past deficits, minus surpluses.

The two are easy to mix up. A deficit is a flow, like water into a bath, and the debt is the water in the bath. Economists usually measure both as a **share of GDP**, which compares them with the size of the economy, the same way a bank compares a loan with your income. The US debt held by the public was 99% of GDP at the end of 2025, and the Congressional Budget Office (CBO) projects it will reach 120% by 2036 ([CBO](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/61882)).

## How governments fund their debt

Taxes pay for most of what a government spends. To cover the rest, it borrows, and it almost always does so by selling **[government bonds](/Macro-Investing-Textbook/glossary/#government-bond)**. A bond is an IOU: the buyer lends the government money, receives interest, and gets the money back on a set date.

### What the government sells

The US Treasury sells several kinds of security, from a few weeks to 30 years ([TreasuryDirect](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/marketable-securities/)):

| Security | Time until it is repaid |
|---|---|
| Treasury bills | 4 to 52 weeks |
| Treasury notes | 2, 3, 5, 7 or 10 years |
| Treasury bonds | 20 or 30 years |
| TIPS (protected against inflation) | 5, 10 or 30 years |
| Floating rate notes | 2 years |

Other governments do the same under other names. In the UK they are called gilts: "a UK Government liability denominated in sterling, issued by HM Treasury" ([UK Debt Management Office](https://www.dmo.gov.uk/responsibilities/gilt-market/about-gilts/)).

### Selling them at auction

The Treasury sells its securities at auctions. Banks, brokers and other investors bid by naming the interest rate they will accept. The Treasury accepts the bids asking for the lowest rates first and works upward until the whole amount is sold. Every winner then gets the same rate. Individuals can also put in a "noncompetitive" bid for up to $10 million and simply accept the rate the auction sets ([TreasuryDirect](https://treasurydirect.gov/auctions/how-auctions-work/)).

So the market, not the government, decides what interest rate the government pays. If investors worry about a country's finances, they bid for higher rates, and borrowing gets more expensive.

### Rolling the debt over

The scale is huge. "In 2025, we held 444 public auctions and issued about $29.7 trillion in Treasury marketable securities," the Treasury says ([TreasuryDirect](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/auctions/)). That is almost as much as all the debt held by the public, $32.8 trillion in November 2026 ([U.S. Treasury](https://fiscaldata.treasury.gov/datasets/debt-to-the-penny/)). Most of that money was not new borrowing. It repaid securities that were coming due, many of them bills that last only weeks.

This is called **[rolling over](/Macro-Investing-Textbook/glossary/#rollover)** the debt: when a bond matures, the government repays it with money from selling a new one. While a government runs deficits it cannot pay its debt off, so it keeps refinancing it. That is why interest rates matter so much. When cheap old debt is replaced with new debt at higher rates, the interest bill rises.

### Who lends to the government

US federal debt on 9 November 2026 ([U.S. Treasury](https://fiscaldata.treasury.gov/datasets/debt-to-the-penny/)):

| Who it is owed to | Amount |
|---|---|
| **The public:** individuals, companies, state and local governments, the Federal Reserve, foreign governments and other investors | $32.76 trillion |
| **The government itself:** mainly trust funds such as Social Security, which invest their surplus cash in special Treasury securities | $7.80 trillion |
| **Total** | **$40.56 trillion** |

Within the public's share, foreign investors and governments held about $9.4 trillion at the start of 2026 ([U.S. Treasury via FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/FDHBFIN)), and the Federal Reserve held about $4.6 trillion on 4 November 2026 ([Federal Reserve via FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/TREAST)). Social Security is the largest of the government's own lenders ([U.S. Treasury](https://fiscaldata.treasury.gov/americas-finance-guide/national-debt/)).

### Why the central bank cannot just pay

A government cannot simply ask its central bank to create the money it needs:

- **United States.** The Fed "does not purchase new Treasury securities directly from the U.S. Treasury." It buys securities that investors already hold, and it says those purchases are not a way of financing the federal deficit ([Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/faqs/how-does-the-federal-reserve-buying-and-selling-of-securities-relate-to-the-borrowing-decisions-of-the-federal-government.htm)).
- **Euro area.** The EU treaties forbid the ECB and the national central banks from giving governments overdrafts or any other credit, and from buying their debt "directly from them" ([Treaty on the Functioning of the EU, article 123](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:12016E123)).

Central banks can still buy government bonds in the market, as the Fed did during quantitative easing (see [Chapter 4](/Macro-Investing-Textbook/part-2-policy/04-central-banks/)). That is why the Fed holds trillions of dollars of Treasuries. But the government has to sell its bonds to investors first.

## How fiscal policy steers the economy

**Automatic stabilisers.** Some of it needs no decision at all. In a recession people earn less, so they pay less tax, and more of them claim unemployment insurance and other benefits. Revenues fall and spending rises on their own, which props up demand. The CBO estimated these automatic effects increased the US deficit by 1.6% of potential GDP in 2020 ([CBO](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/60970)).

**Deliberate stimulus.** When the automatic stabilisers are not enough, governments pass new spending or tax cuts, as in the CARES Act. This is faster and bigger, but it needs a vote, so it comes with a delay and a political fight.

**Tightening.** In a boom, the government can do the reverse: raise taxes or cut spending to cool demand. That is politically much harder than the stimulus, which is one reason debt tends to rise over time.

Fiscal policy and monetary policy work together, and sometimes against each other. In 2020 the Fed cut rates to near zero and bought bonds (see [Chapter 4](/Macro-Investing-Textbook/part-2-policy/04-central-banks/)) at the same time as the government spent trillions. Together they were much stronger than either alone.

## The debt snowball: interest versus growth

Can a country borrow forever? What matters is not the size of the debt but how it moves *relative to the economy*. Three things decide it ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/tnm/2010/tnm1002.pdf)):

1. **The interest rate** on the debt makes it grow. A higher rate adds more each year.
2. **Economic growth** makes the economy bigger, so the same debt is a smaller share of it.
3. **The deficit before interest** (called the *primary deficit*) adds new borrowing on top.

When the interest rate is higher than the growth rate, debt grows on its own, even if the government stops borrowing anything new. That is the snowball. When growth beats the interest rate, the debt shrinks as a share of GDP without any budget surplus. The IMF says that persistent deficits, averaging around 5% of GDP worldwide, are the main driver of rising public debt, and that a widening gap between interest rates and growth is making it worse ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/fiscal-monitor/2026/april/english/execsumch1-combined.pdf)).

Debt climbed through the 1980s and early 1990s, dipped as the decade ended (the budget was in surplus in 2000), then rose sharply after 2008 and 2020. The CBO projects a deficit of about 5.8% of GDP in 2026, against an average of 3.8% over the past 50 years ([CBO](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/61882)). The same report says interest on the debt goes from 3.3% of GDP in 2026 to 4.6% in 2036, and that rising interest costs drive much of the increase in deficits ([CBO](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/61882)). That is the snowball at work: the bigger the debt and the higher the rates, the more of each year's budget goes to interest, which adds to the next year's deficit.

For an investor, this is why debt matters. Governments that borrow heavily depend on bond buyers to keep lending. If those buyers demand higher interest, the government's costs rise, and it may have to cut spending, raise taxes or lean on its central bank. Those choices move currencies, bond yields and stock markets, which is exactly where macro investors look for trades.

## Key takeaways

- **Fiscal policy** is the government using taxes and spending to steer the economy. A **deficit** is one year's borrowing, and **debt** is the total pile.
- Governments fund their debt by **selling bonds at auction**, where investors' bids set the interest rate. They **roll over** maturing bonds by selling new ones, and in the US and the euro area the central bank may not buy new government debt directly.
- **Automatic stabilisers** cushion recessions without any new decision. **Stimulus** adds more, but needs a vote and comes with delay.
- Whether debt is safe depends on **interest versus growth**, plus the **deficit before interest**. When interest beats growth, debt snowballs.
- Fiscal and monetary policy work together. In 2020 they both pushed in the same direction.
- Rising debt can push up interest costs, which is why bond markets watch government budgets closely.
